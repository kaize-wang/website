# Huang, Kulkarni & Swaminathan (2008) — Core Reference

> A compact research-use reference prepared for the accompanying reading note. It paraphrases the paper and retains the mathematical objects needed for cross-reading. It is not a reproduction of the publisher PDF or the full electronic companion.

**Paper:** Wei Huang, Vidyadhar Kulkarni, and Jayashankar M. Swaminathan, “Managing the Inventory of an Item with a Replacement Warranty,” *Management Science* 54(8), 1441–1452 (2008). DOI: 10.1287/mnsc.1080.0863.

## 1. Problem in one sentence

A firm must jointly plan inventory for ordinary new-product demand and replacement demand generated by products that fail while still under renewable warranty. The key informational state is therefore not inventory alone: the age distribution of units currently under warranty also matters.

## 2. State, demand, and decision

At period $n$:

- $x_n$: net inventory before replenishment; negative values represent backlog.
- $y_n \ge x_n$: post-order inventory position / order-up-to decision.
- $w_n=(w_n^1,\ldots,w_n^K)$: numbers of units of each warranty age.
- $\zeta_n$: new-product demand, with cdf $F_n$ and density $f_n$.
- $\beta=(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_K)$: age-dependent failure fractions.

Warranty-replacement demand generated by the current installed base is

$$
\beta\cdot w_n=\sum_{j=1}^K \beta_j w_n^j.
$$

Hence total period demand is new demand plus warranty replacement demand.

## 3. Backlogging model: state transition

The paper's key feedback mechanism is that fulfilled demand creates new age-1 warranty exposure. With $[x]^-=-\min\{x,0\}$,

$$
w_{n+1}^1=\min\{y_n,\,\beta\cdot w_n+\zeta_n\}+[x_n]^-,
$$

$$
w_{n+1}^j=w_n^{j-1}(1-\beta_{j-1}),\qquad j=2,\ldots,K,
$$

and

$$
x_{n+1}=y_n-\zeta_n-\beta\cdot w_n.
$$

This is why inventory decisions affect future demand: what is sold or replaced today enters the future warranty-age state.

## 4. One-period cost

Let $c$ be unit procurement cost, $h$ holding cost, and $p$ shortage/backlog cost. The expected holding-and-shortage term is $L_n(w,y)$, and total expected one-period cost after ordering to $y$ is

$$
C_n(w,x,y)=c(y-x)+L_n(w,y).
$$

For $y\ge \beta\cdot w$, $L_n$ has the familiar newsvendor split: holding cost integrates over realizations below $y-\beta\cdot w$, while shortage cost integrates over realizations above it. When $y<\beta\cdot w$, shortage is unavoidable even before new demand is realized.

## 5. Finite-horizon dynamic program

Let $V_{n,N}(w,x)$ be the optimal expected discounted cost from period $n$ through terminal time $N$, and let $G_{n,N}(w,x,y)$ be the cost if the first decision is to order to $y$.

The recursion is

$$
V_{n,N}(w,x)=\min_{y\ge x}G_{n,N}(w,x,y),
$$

with terminal condition

$$
V_{N,N}(w,x)=T(w,x).
$$

The continuation value separates the realizations $\zeta\le y-\beta\cdot w$ and $\zeta>y-\beta\cdot w$, because the next warranty state differs depending on whether all current demand can be fulfilled.

## 6. Terminal warranty liability

For a unit currently at warranty age $i$, let $m_i$ denote the expected discounted future warranty cost generated by that unit. The paper obtains these quantities recursively from the age-specific failure fractions.

A terminal cost that internalizes future warranty obligations is

$$
T(w,x)=m\cdot w+\alpha m_1[x]^- -cx,
$$

where $m=(m_1,\ldots,m_K)$ and $\alpha$ is the discount factor.

Interpretation:

- $m\cdot w$ prices the future warranty liability of units already in the field;
- $\alpha m_1[x]^-$ prices the warranty liability associated with backlog eventually served;
- $-cx$ values leftover inventory / terminal backlog at procurement cost.

This special terminal cost is central to the structural proof.

## 7. Modified shortage cost and order-up-to target

Define

$$
\bar p=p-(1-\alpha)m_1.
$$

The finite-horizon base-stock target is

$$
S_n(w)=\beta\cdot w+F_n^{-1}\!\left(
\frac{\bar p-c(1-\alpha)}{\bar p+h}
\right).
$$

The first term, $\beta\cdot w$, is the direct adjustment for expected warranty replacements. The quantile term is a newsvendor-type target using a shortage penalty corrected for future warranty liability.

## 8. Main finite-horizon structural result

Under the paper's stochastic-ordering assumption on demand distributions, the period-$n$ objective can be decomposed so that the relevant part is minimized at $S_n(w)$ and the residual term does not create a lower minimizer above that point.

The operational conclusion is:

$$
y_n^*=\max\{x_n,\,S_n(w_n)\}.
$$

So the optimal policy is a **$w$-dependent base-stock policy**: the target inventory level changes with the warranty-age vector.

### IID special case

If new demand is identically distributed across periods, $F_n=F$, the target becomes stationary:

$$
S(w)=\beta\cdot w+F^{-1}\!\left(
\frac{\bar p-c(1-\alpha)}{\bar p+h}
\right).
$$

The same stationary $w$-dependent structure then carries from the finite-horizon problem to the infinite-horizon discounted problem.

## 9. Infinite-horizon discounted cost

The paper uses the finite-horizon result plus a vanishing terminal-value argument. Under the stationary policy, the expected warranty-age state is controlled through a substochastic aging/failure matrix $P$, with $P^N\to0$. Consequently,

$$
\alpha^N\,\mathbb E[T(w_N,x_N)]\to0,
$$

which allows the finite-horizon optimal values and stationary-policy values to converge to the same infinite-horizon discounted value.

## 10. Infinite-horizon average cost

For the undiscounted case $\alpha=1$, the stationary target simplifies to

$$
S(w)=\beta\cdot w+F^{-1}\!\left(\frac{p}{p+h}\right).
$$

The paper establishes positive recurrence of the state process under this policy and then shows that the long-run average cost is well defined. The same $w$-dependent base-stock policy minimizes the infinite-horizon average cost.

## 11. Emergency-supply variant

When unmet demand is filled immediately by an emergency source instead of being backlogged, the next inventory is nonnegative and the state recursion changes accordingly. The optimal finite-horizon order-up-to level becomes

$$
S_n(w)=\beta\cdot w+F_n^{-1}\!\left(\frac{p-c}{p+h-c}\right),
$$

where $p$ is now the emergency-purchase cost. With IID demand, this again becomes a stationary $w$-dependent policy.

## 12. Extensions

The paper studies three directions beyond the main free-replacement fixed-warranty model.

### 12.1 Pro-rata renewable warranty

A customer pays an age-dependent amount when a failed item is replaced. This changes the future warranty-cost quantities $m_i$ and therefore shifts the optimal base-stock target. The structural logic of the earlier results survives after replacing the free-replacement warranty-cost terms by their pro-rata counterparts.

### 12.2 Warranty expiration / survival uncertainty

Instead of every item remaining under warranty deterministically until age $K$, a random fraction of units can remain covered from one age to the next. The warranty-age transition is therefore randomized rather than fixed.

### 12.3 More general failure behavior

The paper also discusses relaxing the fixed proportional-failure specification. This points toward models in which warranty claims are driven by richer failure processes rather than deterministic age-specific fractions.

## 13. Computational study

The numerical experiment compares:

1. the integrated policy that uses warranty information;
2. a current/ad-hoc policy that plans only for new demand;
3. an approximation that does not use complete warranty-age information.

The study uses 343 parameter combinations, with $c=2$, $K=20$, $\alpha=0.95$, multiple values of $h$, $p$, and the first-age failure fraction, IID uniform new demand, a 100-period simulation horizon, and 1,000 replications per instance.

Main findings reported in the paper:

- Integrated planning reduces cost by about **69.33% on average** relative to planning only for new demand.
- The maximum improvement in those experiments is about **86.55%**.
- Complete warranty-age information adds a smaller incremental gain over the smart approximation: roughly **4% on average**, with gains reaching about **13%** in the reported parameter grid.

The economic message is therefore two-layered: accounting for warranty demand matters a great deal; perfect age information is useful, but much of the value can already be captured by a good approximation.

## 14. Electronic Companion: proof roadmap

The electronic companion supplies the omitted technical proofs.

### Theorem 1

The proof is by backward induction.

- **Claim 1:** verify the three structural statements at period $N-1$ using the special terminal cost.
- **Claim 2:** assuming the statements hold at period $n$, propagate them to period $n-1$.
- The decomposition of $G_{n,N}$ isolates a newsvendor-like convex component and an additional term that is flat below the target and increasing above it. This is what prevents nonconvexity in the full state-dependent objective from destroying the base-stock minimizer.

### Proposition 2

The companion proves that the state process under the stationary base-stock policy is irreducible, aperiodic, and positive recurrent. The key device is a Foster-Lyapunov drift argument using the warranty-aging matrix and the boundedness induced by the order-up-to policy.

### Proposition 3

The proof bounds expected one-period costs uniformly over time for a fixed initial state. Combined with positive recurrence, this yields existence of the relevant long-run average-cost limit.

## 16. What to compare against the reading note

When reading side by side, the most useful checkpoints are:

1. Why $w_n$ is a genuine state variable rather than metadata.
2. How today's fulfillment creates tomorrow's warranty exposure.
3. Why the terminal cost is engineered to make the finite-horizon structural proof work.
4. Why $S_n(w)$ separates into a direct warranty-demand adjustment $\beta\cdot w$ plus a newsvendor quantile.
5. Which assumptions are structural (renewability, proportional failures, no lead time) and which are mainly used for tractability.
6. How the electronic-companion induction handles the fact that the full objective need not be globally convex in $y$.
